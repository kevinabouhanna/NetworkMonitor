Connections

Which companies your apps send data to, from which apps, in which countries, and to which servers. It's a section of the main window: open it from Open Connections under Connections in the popover, the four squares beside the popover's gear, Connections… (⌘K) in the right-click menu, or the sidebar. Its settings, behind the gear in its toolbar, also set the layout it opens with.

It runs quietly: until you make a rule or choose Ask Me (see Blocking), nothing pops up and nothing is blocked. NetworkMonitor records the servers each app talks to as it already counts their data, and Connections shows them whenever you look.

Reading the window

The toolbar holds the view (List | Both | Map), the period (Day, Week, Month, Year) in the middle, ‹ Today ›, search (⌘F) and the gear. The row under it says what's shown (Traffic or Blocked), what the list is grouped by and narrowed to, and which networks it covers. Then the period's name, its total, and the counts.

  • Group by Companies, Apps or Countries. Companies is the default, because that's the question: who gets your data.
  • Open a group to see what's inside it. A company opens into the apps that talk to it, the countries its servers are in, and the servers themselves. An app opens into its companies, countries and servers. A country opens into its companies, apps and servers.
  • Click a row inside a group to narrow everything to it. Choosing Teams inside Microsoft reads Microsoft › Teams, and each chip's ✕ undoes its part.
  • Drag across the timeline, or click a bar, to see one part of the day: what your apps contacted in the hour after a call, say. Click the same bar again, or the chip's ✕, for the whole period.
  • Search, the magnifying glass in the toolbar, finds a company, a network's registered name, an app, a country or an address.
  • Day, Week, Month, Year, the arrows and ⌘← ⌘→ move through time, as in every section, and Networks: All / Wi-Fi / Hotspot narrows it to one kind of network.
  • Under each app is who made it, read from its code signature, and the companies it talks to besides its maker. Hosts aren't counted: an app on Amazon's network isn't talking to Amazon.

The map

Connections shows the list, the map, or both side by side (List | Both | Map). Both is the default, and whichever you choose is remembered. Too narrow for both, it shows the list until you widen the window.

In a narrow window, a row's bar shortens before its name does, here and in Usage History's and Ads and Trackers' Both (#534). A tag beside a name (ALWAYS ALLOWED, ADS, METERED) becomes its icon before the name is cut short; hover it, or ask VoiceOver, for its word.

  • Countries are shaded by how much data went there, on a logarithmic scale, so a country with a few kilobytes still shows beside one with gigabytes.
  • Hover a country for a card with its top companies. Click it to narrow everything to it.
  • Narrow to a company, app or server, and lines run from your country to where its data went. Your country is the first of these that knows (#231): Location, when NetworkMonitor already has it for Wi-Fi names (it never asks for it just for this); your Mac's own internet address, when it holds one the internet sees, such as an IPv6 address; or your Mac's Region setting. Hover the ring where the lines start to see which: Your Mac, in Lebanon (from Location). Nothing is looked up online: the place Location gives is turned into a country with the map's own outlines, and only the country is kept.
  • Lines pulse to the countries your apps reach right now whenever the map shows traffic for a period that includes now, with no switch to turn on (#182). Only the pulses redraw on their own, and nothing runs while Connections isn't on screen (another section, or the window closed, minimised or covered), while it shows Blocked or a past period, or while nothing is answering.
  • Drag to move, pinch or use the buttons to zoom. Places too small to see at the current zoom, and places drawn inside another country such as Réunion, show as a dot when something went there.
  • Cloudflare, Fastly and similar networks aren't placed. They answer each address from whichever data centre is nearest, so no one country is where the data went. The corner of the map says how much went to them.

The outlines are Natural Earth's (public domain), drawn by the app. No map service is used and nothing is downloaded.

What the names mean

A server is an address. NetworkMonitor names it from data shipped inside the app: the country DB-IP places the address in, and the network it belongs to. Nothing is looked up online. The data is refreshed once a month, the same signed file for everyone, never on a hotspot; Update them monthly in Connections' settings switches that off, and the month in use is shown there (#121). See privacy.

  • A company is the owner of the network the server is on. That's right for companies that run their own networks: Google, Microsoft, Meta, Apple and many more.
  • Hosted on Amazon (or Cloudflare, Akamai, Fastly, Google Cloud and others), marked HOST, means the server is on a network that carries thousands of companies' traffic. The data can't tell which one, so NetworkMonitor doesn't guess.
  • Unknown network is an address the data doesn't list: a private, carrier or VPN address such as Tailscale's.
  • Other servers is what an app talked to past 500 servers in one hour, added up as one. Only peer-to-peer apps and large calls get there.
  • A country is where DB-IP places the address. It isn't where the company is, or where data ends up. For networks that answer one address from many places at once, such as Cloudflare's, the server that answered is usually much nearer.

Website names

Without help, a server is only an address, and anything on a cloud's network reads as Hosted on Amazon. With See which websites your apps visit turned on, a content filter, the part of macOS made for apps like this one, tells NetworkMonitor the name each app connected by. Then wss-primary.slack.com on Amazon's network reads as Slack, and the Servers list shows names, with the address underneath.

  • Offered when you first open NetworkMonitor, in the welcome window, and optional. Said no? Turn On… is in Connections' settings, and above the list in Connections until you close it there (#134).
  • macOS asks twice: once in System Settings (General › Login Items & Extensions › Network Extensions ⓘ), then once in a dialog. The row shows each step while it waits.
  • It runs in the background from then on, with nothing to manage.
  • It blocks only what you block (see below).
  • It fails open: if the filter stops, nothing is cut off.
  • Once it's on, the settings say nothing about it (#181). It's switched off in System Settings › Network › Filters, and then Connections' settings offer Turn On… again. Uninstall removes it.
  • Apps that connect by address are named from the handshake (#196). Chrome, Figma and other Chromium apps look addresses up themselves, often over encrypted DNS, so the filter gets no name from them. For those connections to port 443 it reads the site's name from the opening handshake, TLS's or QUIC's, which is sent for the network to read. Only that name: nothing after it, and nothing of a connection that already has a name.
  • What still reads by its network's owner: a connection on another port, one that doesn't use TLS or QUIC, and a site that hides its name with Encrypted Client Hello, which shows its provider's public name instead, such as cloudflare-ech.com.

Blocking

Right-click a company, app, country or server in Connections and choose Block, Block on Hotspots or Always Allow (#114). The row says so beside its name, and Connections' settings list every rule, each with a button to take it back.

  • A server with a name is ruled by name (#197), since a CDN moves a name from address to address. Each choice opens onto the name itself or its whole site: stats.wp.com, or All of *.wp.com. A rule for a name covers every name under it too. A server without a name is ruled by its address, as before.
  • The narrowest rule wins: a server's address, then a site (the longer name first), then an app, then a company, then a country. So Block Meta with Always Allow WhatsApp lets WhatsApp through, Block United States with Always Allow Apple lets Apple's servers there through, and Block Google with Always Allow *.youtube.com lets YouTube through.
  • A connection that opens without a name is decided once its handshake names it (#196, #197): Chrome's, for example. Its first bytes wait for that, and a name never found leaves it decided as it was without one.
  • On Hotspots means while the Mac is on a hotspot, as the Network page judges it.
  • On "this network" (#200): the menu also offers Block "…" on "Office Wi-Fi", for the network the Mac is on. The rule applies only there, keyed by the router's identity as Usage History tells networks apart, never by the Wi-Fi name; elsewhere, the next rule decides. Its badge names the network, and a rule for everywhere replaces it.
  • With Hotspot mode on, every app's updater waits on a hotspot (#98): a program inside an app named like an updater (opera_autoupdate, Raycast's Updater, Sparkle's), Sparkle's downloader, or an updater of its own (GoogleUpdater, Microsoft AutoUpdate, Squirrel's ShipIt), and the update-only servers Hotspot mode's helper blocks. Your rules come first, so Always Allow lets one through, and macOS and Apple's apps follow Hotspot mode's system setting instead. What it stopped shows under Blocked.
  • Never blocked: the local network, looking up names (DNS), the clock, and getting an address. Without them nothing else works.
  • Export… and Import… beside the rules in Connections' settings (#201) save every rule to a JSON file, for a backup or another Mac. Import adds the rules that aren't there yet, never replaces one that is, and says how many it added.
  • Block Everything, in the menu bar item's right-click menu (#198), stops every app's new connections at once, Apple's included and whatever your rules allow, until you choose it again. Only the essentials above and NetworkMonitor itself go through. While it's on, the readout turns red and the popover says so, with Turn Off. It lasts across a relaunch and a restart. Connections already open carry on until they close.
  • A rule about an app follows its signature (#202): it remembers the team that signed the app when it was made, and doesn't apply to a program at that path signed by anyone else, so swapping in another program doesn't inherit the app's rules. Under Ask Me, that program is asked about afresh. Apps with no team (Apple's own, ad-hoc or self-signed ones) are matched by path, as before. An update signed by the same team carries on.
  • An app is the app and the programs it starts, as Connections lists them: a rule on iTerm covers the curl you run in it, unless curl has a rule of its own. Safari's pages load through a WebKit process, and a rule on either catches them.
  • From the next connection: a connection already open carries on until it closes.
  • The filter keeps its own copy of the rules, so they apply with the app quit and after a restart, before the app is back.
  • Blocking needs website names on. Choosing Block while they're off starts turning them on, with the steps above the list.

Ads and trackers

Block ads and trackers (#176) blocks, in every app, the sites on two lists. Ad blocking is a section of its own in the main window, Ads and Trackers, and its settings, behind the gear in its toolbar, hold the switch, Update the lists weekly, and Lists and Your Rules.

List What it holds From Licence
Ads and trackers about 77,000 ad, tracking and analytics servers StevenBlack's unified hosts MIT
Malware and phishing about 1,900 servers spreading malware now URLhaus, by malware-filter CC0
  • Your rules come first. Always Allow on a site or a server in Connections lets it through, and a rule that blocks still blocks. Always Allow on an app, a company or a country (Ask Me's answers too, and the apps you allow under Only Where I Allow) lets it connect, and the lists still apply to it (#316): allowing Google in Chrome doesn't let doubleclick.net through.
  • A listed name covers every name under it, down to its site: listing doubleclick.net blocks static.doubleclick.net. A list can never block a whole suffix such as com, gov.lb or myshopify.com. A name's site comes from the Public Suffix List (#318), as browsers judge it: moph.gov.lb and shop.myshopify.com are sites of their own.
  • It works for apps that connect by address too, such as Chrome, through the name in the connection's handshake (#196).
  • It sees through a site's own name for a tracker (#255): metrics.example.com that is an alias of a listed tracker, as the answer to the Mac's lookup says, is blocked as that tracker. The answers are read, never changed, over UDP or TCP, and an alias is kept as long as its answer says, up to a day. Your rules meet an alias as they meet the name (#321): always block omtrdc.net stops metrics.example.com too, and never block for every app or one keeps an alias from counting for the ad lists. Lookups an app encrypts itself, such as Chrome's Secure DNS, can't be read.
  • Connections tags each listed server ADS or MALWARE, whether the lists are on or not, so it shows what's tracking you either way. Show: Blocked lists what they stopped.
  • The popover's Ads blocked button counts them for its period, by site or by app, in red; Open Ads and Trackers (⌘B) at the bottom of its view opens the section.
  • What it can't block: ads served from a site's own servers, like YouTube's, since they can't be told apart from the site; empty boxes in a page where an ad would have been; and anything without a name (another port, or not TLS or QUIC). It's not an ad blocker for the browser.
  • The lists stay current by themselves. A workflow rebuilds them every Monday, refuses a list that shrank (below a fixed floor, or to under half of last week's, #319) or that catches a site people use every day, and signs them; the app fetches them weekly while they're on, never on a hotspot (Update them weekly). It ships with a copy, so they work offline.

The Ads and Trackers section

A section of the main window (#251, #436), from Open Ads and Trackers under Ads blocked in the popover, the four squares beside the popover's gear, Ads and Trackers… in the right-click menu, the sidebar, or ⌘B while a window is open. Overview | Log heads it, and the toolbar's search (⌘F) covers both.

Overview:

  • How many were blocked in the period (a day, week, month or year, with ‹ Today ›). The ⓘ beside the total says how it's counted, and what each list blocked, largest first (#402). A list switched off since keeps what it blocked while it was on, marked off. The period is in the toolbar on Overview only: the Log is the present, so it's hidden there (#311).
  • List, Both or Chart, at the start of the toolbar, as Connections has List, Both or Map (#524): the list alone, the list on the left and the chart on the right (the default, side by side as Connections has its list and map, #534), or the chart filling the window. Whichever you choose is remembered, and it's hidden on Log with the period.
  • A chart of blocks over the period, by hour, day or month, drawn and read as Usage History's (#536). Hover a bar for its time and count. Click one to narrow the list and the total to that hour or day, as a timeline bar does in Connections: its chip above the list, a click on the bar again, or Escape shows the whole period again.
  • Sites or Apps, at the end of the row under the toolbar, where Connections and Usage History have All, Wi-Fi or Hotspot: Sites, by the part of the name a person would call the site (doubleclick.net), with the names under it that were blocked; or Apps, with the sites each tried most. One at a time, each list has the window's width (#524). The rows are Usage History's (#536):
    • Click a row to choose it: the chart shows its share of each bar, the bar's own colour under the rest, and the hovered bar's count for it. Its chip, a click on it again, or Escape (after a picked bar's) lets it go.
    • The chevron opens a site's apps, the apps that tried to reach it, or an app's sites, each with its count and the hour it was last blocked; the first eight, then Show all. Click one inside to see that site in that app on the chart.
    • Right-click a row, as in the Log: never or always block its site, in every app or only its app, stop filtering its app, remove a rule you made about it, or copy its name.
    • The keyboard, once a row is clicked (or the list is reached with Tab): ↑ and ↓ choose the row above or below, → opens a row, ← closes it or goes back up to the row it's in, and Space or Return chooses the row again once it's let go.
  • Pause: for an hour or until tomorrow, in every app. The lists stay chosen, the filter is sent none until then, and the popover says it's paused. Kept across a relaunch. Whenever on this network (#253) turns ad blocking off on the network the Mac is on, by the router's identity as History knows it, every time it's there; Your Rules lists those networks, and Turn On Here undoes it.

Log (#252), beside Overview: every connection the filter decides, live, newest first, with the time, the app, the site (or its address, marked No Name when nothing named it), Blocked or Let through, and why in plain words (On the ads and trackers list, Your rule: never block example.com, Let through, though it's on a list). Show All, Blocked, On a List or No Name, and search. Right-click a row to never or always block its name or its whole site, in every app or only that one, to stop filtering its app, or to remove a rule you made. Pause holds the list still, Clear empties it, and Export… saves what's shown as text. It keeps the last 5,000 in memory only; lookups aren't listed. It fills once ad blocking or a rule is on, and starts afresh each time NetworkMonitor opens.

Lists (#254), in Ads and Trackers' settings: which lists block, and what each blocked today (#311).

  • Built in: the two above, switched on and off here.

  • Recommended (#314), on by default: HaGeZi Normal and OISD Big, chosen by the app so nobody has to weigh lists against broken sites. Between them they block most ads and trackers; OISD is made to block ads without breaking sites and HaGeZi Normal is HaGeZi's all-round tier, below Pro, and the pair is a common low-breakage setup. They overlap rather than conflict. One switch for the pair; in Recommended the lists in use follow ListCatalogue.recommended, so a later version can change it. Installs that had chosen lists before #314 keep their choice.

  • Advanced, collapsed while Recommended is on: every list, and Add List. Switching a list here, or adding one, makes it your own choice, starting from the lists in use; switching Recommended back on keeps your choice for when you switch it off again.

  • More lists, under Advanced, downloaded on the Mac from where their makers publish them, by topic:

    Topic Lists
    More ad and tracker lists HaGeZi Light, Normal and Pro; OISD Small and Big; AdGuard DNS filter; Peter Lowe's list
    Trackers EasyPrivacy, as Firebog extracts it
    Pop-ups HaGeZi Pop-up Ads
    Malware and phishing HaGeZi Threat Intelligence

    No list that blocks encrypted DNS is offered: it would stop Private Relay and browsers' own, and let the internet provider see the sites (#246, #287). Hover a list for who keeps it and its licence. They're downloaded rather than shipped, so the GPL ones are fine to offer; the signed blocklists release keeps only lists we may republish.

  • Add List takes any list's https:// address: a hosts file, a plain list of names, or Adblock rules about whole sites (||site^, with $important; @@||site^ and $badfilter are exceptions). Rules about parts of pages are skipped; a list with nothing else is refused. It's named from its Title: line, or its site.

  • Each switched-on list is downloaded weekly while ad blocking is on, never on a hotspot, a metered connection or in Low Data Mode, and the last good copy is kept. A row says how many sites it has and when it was updated, or why it isn't there. Right-click for Update Now, Copy Address, or Remove for one you added.

  • They block only alongside a built-in list, after it: a site on StevenBlack's list counts as Ads and trackers. The Overview and the Log name the list that blocked each connection, so one that blocks too much can be found and switched off.

  • Import… under Your Rules switches on the lists a uBlock Origin backup or AdGuard's settings had that are here (Peter Lowe's, EasyPrivacy, AdGuard DNS filter), and adds the ones they had by address.

Your Rules (#253), beside Lists in Ads and Trackers' settings: your own say over what the lists do.

  • Never block a site, and every name under it: it goes through though a list has it.
  • Always block a site that no list has.
  • Either for every app or one app, from the menu beside the site. One app's rule follows its signature, as firewall rules do (#320): a program at that path signed by another team isn't the app, so it doesn't inherit don't filter.
  • Don't filter an app: the ad and tracker lists leave all its connections alone, for an app that breaks.
  • Malware lists still apply (#317): never block and don't filter change what the ad, tracker and pop-up lists do, and the built-in malware list and the downloaded ones in Malware and phishing block whatever they say. To let such a site through anyway, Always Allow it in Connections. The Overview doesn't offer Never Block for a site only they blocked.
  • Type a site, or paste an address or a rule (https://ads.example.com/x, *.example.com, ||example.com^): the name is read out of it. Return makes it never block.
  • Right-click a site in the Overview to never block it, or an app to stop filtering it.
  • Import… brings rules over from uBlock Origin (its backup file: trusted sites, My filters, and the lists it had on), AdGuard (its exported settings: the allowlist, user rules and its filters), or rules in Adblock syntax, chosen as a file or pasted. A summary comes first, and nothing changes until you accept it:
    • ||site^ is always block, @@||site^ never block, $app= (a bundle ID, an app's name or path) makes it one app's, and @@*$app=… is don't filter. Hosts-file lines and bare names are always block.
    • Trusted sites and the allowlist become never block. There they mean "block nothing on this site's pages"; here, connections to the site itself go through, since the filter can't tell which page asked.
    • The lists it had on switch on the ones NetworkMonitor has (ads and trackers, malware, and those under Lists), lists it had added by address are added the same way (#254), and the rest are named as not offered yet.
    • Rules that hide parts of a page (##, scriptlets) are counted as waiting for the browser extension (#257); rules about part of a site (a path, $third-party, $domain=) as waiting for Enhanced ad blocking (#256); anything else is listed with why it can't be used.
  • Export… saves your rules in the same syntax, which uBlock Origin's My filters and AdGuard's user rules take, and Import reads back.

They change only what the lists do, and only while ad blocking is on: a firewall rule in Connections still comes first, and never block lets nothing through that your rules or mode would stop. Decided in this order: one app's never block, one app's always block, every app's never block, every app's always block, then the lists, with the longer name first within each. What always block stops counts with the lists, as Your Rules.

The overview reads list_blocks in History (#250), so it starts from when that was added, is kept as long as History, and Clear History clears it.

Show: Blocked, beside Group by, lists what was blocked instead of what was sent: the same period, grouping, search and map, counting connections rather than bytes. Right-click one to allow it.

Let apps connect, in Connections' settings, is one of three:

  • Anywhere, the default: everything connects except what you block.
  • Ask Me (#129): the first time an app connects to a company, it waits while a small panel at the top right asks. On a host's network, such as Amazon's or Cloudflare's, where the owner says nothing, it asks about the site instead (#199).
    • The panel names the site (#199): Figma wants to connect to api.segment.io, then whose it is, the country and the port. For an app that connects by address, such as Chrome, the name comes from the connection's handshake, read before asking.
    • For: the site (segment.io and every name under it) or the whole company.
    • Allow: Once lets it go and asks again in an hour; Until It Quits lasts until that app quits; Always makes a rule. Block makes a rule. Rules from answers are for that app only and listed in Connections' settings ("*.segment.io · for Figma, until it quits"). The Allow button's menu also offers the app anywhere.
    • Every connection between the same app and company or site waits on the one question, and with no answer in 30 seconds they go ahead, and the panel says so. Nothing is asked about macOS or Apple's apps, or while NetworkMonitor isn't running.
  • Only Where I Allow: every app is blocked until you allow it, apart from macOS and Apple's own apps, which can always connect unless you block them. It asks first, since that includes your browser, and Connections then opens on Blocked, where you allow each app or company with a right-click.

A rule for one app comes before any rule for every app: Amazon · for Slack allowed with Hosted on Amazon blocked lets Slack through and nothing else.

Encrypted lookups

Encrypt lookups, under Lookups in Connections' settings (#291), off until switched on, sends the Mac's DNS lookups encrypted, over DNS over HTTPS, to Cloudflare (1.1.1.1), Quad9 (9.9.9.9) or Google (8.8.8.8). Without it they travel unencrypted to the network's DNS server, so the internet provider and anyone on the Wi-Fi can read every name looked up.

  • It needs the content filter, whose system extension carries it as a DNS proxy. macOS asks once to allow it.
  • Answers come back unchanged, and the filter still reads their aliases (#255).
  • Names only the network knows stay on it: single names like router, .local, .lan, .home, .home.arpa, .internal, .corp, and reverse lookups of local addresses.
  • It fails towards working. A lookup the resolver doesn't answer in 2.5 s goes to the network's DNS server, as it would have; after three in a row, every lookup does for a minute. Lookups over TCP, which are rare, go to the network's server unchanged.
  • It checks itself. Switched on, or at launch with it on, the app looks up a name that exists nowhere: an answer, even no such name, means lookups work. If none comes in three tries while the router answers, it switches itself off again and says why under the switch.
  • Updates switch it off and on again around replacing the extension, with the filter (#278).
  • Apps that encrypt their own lookups, like Chrome with Secure DNS, aren't affected; ECH is never touched.

What it costs

Nothing you'd notice. Recording uses rows nettop already reports, and measured no extra CPU. The record is kept as long as Usage History (Keep history for in Usage History's settings) and cleared with it. The data that names servers adds about 5.5 MB to the download.
