Accuracy and battery

The total is the one to trust

Your speed and connection total come straight from the kernel's own byte counters, and run all the time. Checked against the kernel across three independent transfers:

Transfer Recorded vs the kernel's own count
10 MB download 100.2%
50 MB download 100.0%
6.5 MB, sustained over 15 seconds, to look for lost samples 99.6%

The kernel's figure is a little above the payload because it includes TCP, TLS and HTTP overhead, which is real traffic you pay for. The live rate tracks to within 0.4%.

Per-app rows won't quite add up to the total, and shouldn't. The kernel counts everything on the wire, including overhead that belongs to no app; nettop counts what each app's sockets moved. In the 50 MB test, curl was credited with 94.8% of the kernel's figure, and the rest was wire overhead. The final second before you quit is never completed either. So the big total is the one to trust; the rows tell you where it went.

A process that comes and goes between two samples may not get a row. nettop reports the processes alive when it samples, every 1 to 10 seconds depending on the power profile. A socket that opens and closes in between still reaches its process's row, but a process that starts and ends in between may never be named. Its bytes are still in the total, and in History and the CSV they're under Not attributed to an app.

Internet, not your own network

Mirroring your screen to an Apple TV, Time Machine to a NAS, a Plex stream from your own server: they cross your Wi-Fi but cost no internet data, so they're left out. AirPlay through a router alone can move 4 to 13 GB an hour, which would otherwise swamp the day.

Every connection's other end is checked: private addresses (10/8, 172.16/12, 192.168/16, link-local) are your own network; everything else, and anything that can't be read, is internet. Under-reporting is the one error the app can't afford, so ambiguity counts as internet. Architecture has the details, including why multicast is never subtracted and why the total is clamped between the app rows and the kernel's count.

Through a proxy or a VPN

A proxy such as Clash Verge (Mihomo) or sing-box, in its TUN mode or set as the system's proxy, carries every app's traffic, and so does a VPN. macOS then sees one app on the internet, the proxy, and that's all it would show (#356).

NetworkMonitor sees which app sent each connection into the proxy, through its tunnel or its port on your Mac, and counts those bytes for that app. The proxy keeps only what it adds itself: its own connections and its overhead. While it does, Network says Traffic through Clash Verge: Counted for each app. Your total doesn't change: it comes from the network interface, which counts every byte once.

It's never more than the proxy moved, so the apps can't add up to more than before. It starts a second or two after the proxy starts carrying traffic, and stops two minutes after it goes quiet; meanwhile a second copy of nettop runs, about as much CPU again as the first. The servers in Usage History stay the proxy's for its share.

Why it won't match Activity Monitor

Activity Monitor's Network tab counts bytes since each process started, often days ago. NetworkMonitor reports what it saw today, this week or this month, from the period's start, and on the Network page what it saw on the connection you're on, since you joined it or since midnight. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.

Battery

NetworkMonitor samples less often when it can, and never counts less. Both sources report the bytes moved between samples, so sampling less often changes how fresh the figures are, not how complete. There's nothing to configure, and Settings › Sampling says which mode is in force.

Readout updates App list updates While nobody can see the menu bar
Plugged in every 0.5 s every 1 s counters every 5 s
On battery every 1 s every 3 s counters every 10 s
Low Power Mode, plugged in or not every 2 s every 10 s counters every 15 s

"Nobody can see the menu bar" means the display is asleep, the screen is locked, or another user's session is in front. Then the readout isn't drawn at all, and it catches up the moment someone can see it.

Measured on an M1 MacBook Air with its own everyday background traffic, two minutes each, the app and its nettop together:

Share of one CPU core
Plugged in 2.68%
On battery 1.19%
Low Power Mode 0.53%
On battery, with nobody watching 0.47%

Most of the app's own share is drawing the menu bar readout. Over a day, Activity Monitor's 12-hour power figure puts it alongside other menu bar utilities that run all the time.

The drain that was a bug

Early versions cost about 1.4 CPU cores, and switched per-app tracking off on battery because of it. The cost turned out to be a bug, not a price. nettop, the tool macOS provides for per-app numbers, polls its input for keystrokes. It was given an input that's always "ready" (/dev/null), so it spun on an empty loop forever. Given an input that waits properly, the same data cost 0.55% of a core, 256 times less, and arrived just as fast.

The fix also showed that switching tracking off on battery was backwards: it turned attribution off exactly where it matters, on a phone's hotspot. One hotspot session showed 25.4 MB on the interface against 1.1 MB attributed to apps. So per-app tracking now always runs, on every network and on battery.

Development shows how to measure a profile without unplugging.
