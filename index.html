The problem

Your Mac spends data behind your back.

Apps update themselves, sync and download whenever they like. On home broadband you never notice. On a phone’s hotspot you pay for every megabyte, and macOS keeps no running tally of which app spent it.

135 MB

Caught while this page was being made. GoogleUpdater went from 13.9 MB to 135 MB in about two minutes, in the background, on its own.

On home Wi‑Fi that was harmless. On a hotspot it would have been 135 MB of your plan, gone before you knew it had started.

Why it’s so hard to see.

01

Updaters run on their own schedule.

Chrome, VS Code, Microsoft AutoUpdate, apps built on Sparkle, and macOS itself fetch new versions whenever they decide to. Nothing tells you when.

02

One app hides behind a dozen names.

A browser runs a swarm of helper processes. A terminal launches git, ssh and command-line tools. In a raw process list, one app’s traffic is scattered across names you’ve never heard of.

03

Activity Monitor keeps a different clock.

It counts bytes since each process started, often days ago. It can’t tell you what the network you’re on right now has used.

04

Local traffic looks like internet traffic.

AirPlay to an Apple TV, Time Machine to a NAS, Plex from your own server. All of it crosses your Wi‑Fi, and none of it touches your data plan.

05

Hotspots are billed by the gigabyte.

Tethering is metered. If you only find out afterwards, when your carrier sends the warning, the data is already gone.

06

A traffic monitor sees everything.

Whatever watches your connections is in a position to report on them. It should ask for nothing, send nothing, and let you read every line.

The solution

Meet NetworkMonitor.

A small menu bar app that shows your speed live, tells you exactly which apps used this connection and who they talked to, and, if you ask it to, stops them updating while you’re on a hotspot. No Dock icon, no window to manage, no account.

Sub-processes

Credit where it’s due.

Helpers fold into the app that owns them, so Chrome’s dozen helper processes are one Google Chrome row. Commands started from a terminal break out beneath it, each with its own figure.

System

macOS noise, folded away.

Dozens of system daemons collapse into a single System row at the bottom of the list. Open it when you’re curious; ignore it when you’re not.

Sorted live

Biggest first.

Every app gets a bar for its share of the total, and the list re-sorts as apps overtake each other.

Per connection

One total per network.

Join a different Wi‑Fi or hotspot and the count starts from zero. Drop out and rejoin the same network and it picks up where it left off. It also resets at midnight, and survives restarts.

Internet only

Your LAN is free.

AirPlay, Time Machine to a NAS, Plex from your own server: they cross your Wi‑Fi but cost no internet data, so they’re never counted against you.

Hotspot mode

Hotspot on.
Updates off.

One setting. When you join a mobile hotspot, NetworkMonitor switches off your apps’ automatic updates, and switches them back on when you leave. Apps keep working. Chrome still browses, Slack still messages, VS Code still edits. They just don’t download new versions of themselves while you’re paying by the gigabyte.

Knows a hotspot when it joins one.

iPhone Personal Hotspot and Android tethering, over Wi‑Fi or USB, from your phone or someone else’s. Any network you’ve put in Low Data Mode counts too. Settings always tells you what it decided about the connection you’re on, and lets you settle it when it guesses wrong.

These need a small helper that’s part of the app: you switch it on once in System Settings, and updates bring it along. It does exactly five things: pause, restore, turn Low Data Mode on and off, and report. The settings it touches are built into it, it answers only NetworkMonitor, and it can’t be asked to change anything else.

Reaches apps it’s never heard of.

Apps are found by how they update, not from a list, so an Electron app it has never heard of is stopped too, even while it’s open. The same switch puts the hotspot in Low Data Mode, so iCloud, Photos and every app that respects it hold back, whatever you have installed. Settings lists every app it can’t pause, with the reason, and the popover tells you if an update got through on your hotspot anyway, which app, and how much it used. A paused app that updated says so, and every update that got through stays in Usage History with the visit it happened on. With website names on, any app’s updater is also stopped at the network, whatever app it belongs to.

The app’s own setting first.For almost every app it changes the app’s own “check for updates automatically” setting, so a manual Check for Updates… still works: it stops unattended downloads, not you. Canva, Figma and Chrome are the exception: with the helper, their update servers stay out of reach until you leave the hotspot. So do Electron apps whose update folder is locked, and, with website names on, any app’s separate updater program.

Security updates pause too.There’s no reliable way to tell a security patch from a feature release, and pretending otherwise would be worse. Leave the hotspot or turn the setting off and everything resumes.

Puts everything back.Click Uninstall… in Settings › About, or right-click the menu bar item and choose Uninstall NetworkMonitor…. It restores every setting exactly as it found it, then removes the helper and moves the app to the Trash.

Connections

Who your apps talk to.
On a map.

Every app sends its data somewhere. Connections shows which companies, countries and servers each one reached, on a list, a world map, or both, for today or any day, week, month or year. Press ⌘K, or click the globe in the popover.

By company, app or country.

A company opens into the apps that reached it, the countries its servers answered from and the servers themselves. Group by app instead to see everyone one app talks to, or by country. The timeline narrows it to an hour, and Live pulses the map to the countries your apps are reaching right now.

Website names, with your OK.

On its own, a server is only an address, and anything on a cloud reads as Hosted on Amazon. Allow the part of macOS made for apps like this one, and Slack shows up as slack.com instead. It’s offered when NetworkMonitor first opens, and it’s optional.

Block what you choose.

Right-click a company, app, country or server: Block, Block on Hotspots or Always Allow. The narrowest rule wins, so blocking Meta while allowing WhatsApp does what you’d expect. Settings lists every rule, and Show: Blocked lists what was stopped.

Or have it ask.

Choose Ask Me and a small panel asks the first time an app reaches a company: Block, Allow Once or Always Allow. Unanswered in 30 seconds, it goes ahead. Or Only Where I Allow. The local network, looking up names and the clock are never blocked, and macOS is never asked about.

See the Connections pane

Named on your Mac.Countries and network owners come from DB-IP’s free data, shipped inside the app. Nothing is looked up online: asking a service about each address would tell it every server your Mac talks to.

Where, never what.It sees which server each connection goes to and the name the app used for it, never what’s inside. If it stops, nothing is cut off.

Speed test

One click.
A fresh measurement.

Click the gauge in the popover and NetworkMonitor runs the speed test Apple ships with every Mac, against Apple’s nearest servers. Download first, then upload, the way speed tests always have. Every run measures afresh. Nothing is cached.

The figures every speed test shows.

Download and upload, in Mbps or MB/s, the same unit as the menu bar. Ping and jitter on a quiet line, plus ping while downloading and while uploading: the figure that explains why a call lags whenever something else is busy.

Tells you what slowed it down.

NetworkMonitor already knows which apps are using your connection. If one was competing during the test, the result names it instead of leaving you to guess.

Careful on a hotspot.

A test uses real data, typically 50–500 MB. On a metered connection it asks first, and shows what the last test there cost.

Watch it from the menu bar.

While it runs, the readout shows the test in cyan, or other colours if yours are close to cyan, so it’s never taken for live rates. Then it holds the result for a few seconds. Or keep your apps in view: the compact style shows the test as two lines above them.

Privacy

It measures volume.
Never contents.

NetworkMonitor counts how much, and where it went, never what. Nothing about your traffic ever leaves your Mac.

Two permissions, both optional.

Location, only because macOS shares a Wi‑Fi network’s name no other way; it never looks up where you are. And website names, to see and block where apps connect. Both are offered when it first opens, and each can be turned on later. Hotspot mode’s helper is one switch in System Settings, not a password.

Two small downloads, both yours to switch off.

Once a month, the new country and network data, the same file for everyone; once a day, whether there’s a newer version. Both come from GitHub, never on a hotspot, and say nothing about your traffic. Beyond those, only a speed test you start. No analytics, no telemetry, no account.

Stays on your Mac.

Your usage, and the servers your apps reached, live in local files on your Mac that you can delete at any time. Countries and companies are named from data inside the app.
